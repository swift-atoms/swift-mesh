# swift-mesh

Use this package when you need a surface as data: a list of vertices and a list of faces that name them by index, over a vertex type you choose. Start from `Mesh<Vertex>`. Build one from your own vertices and faces, from a grid with `Mesh(columns:rows:seams:vertex:)`, which is how every parametric surface becomes a mesh, or from the ready surfaces `torus`, `sphere`, `box` and `teapot`, from a `Bezier<Vertex>.Patch` from swift-bezier, or from a list of polygons, such as quads, each with vertices of its own. `cells(on:columns:rows:inset:per:including:)` cuts a four-sided face, such as a side of a box, into a grid of cells and gives an inset quad for each cell you choose, or one strip per row as a coarser level of detail. In the plane, `disk(radius:cells:)` is a disk of quads, and `Mesh(inside:around:cells:)` fills an outline drawn from Bézier curves, such as a soundboard's, with quads laid out the same way. `outline` gives the edges only one face uses, where a boundary condition or a rim goes. For a body, `Mesh.Volume` is the solid counterpart: vertices and hexahedral cells, each a `Mesh.Volume.Cell` of eight corners. `Mesh.Volume(below:layers:depth:)` fills the body below any planar mesh of quads, such as a plate's, in columns of cells, as a guitar's box below its top; `Mesh.Volume(under:around:depth:cells:)` fills a bowl under an outline, as a lute's body under its soundboard, and `Mesh.Volume.bowl(radius:cells:)` is the half ball it is carried from. Both keep the plate's mesh as their face at the top, vertex for vertex, so the plate and the air below it can be coupled node to node. `volumes()` gives each cell's volume and `boundary` the closed surface of faces only one cell uses. When the vertices are `Point<3, Scalar>` from swift-point, `areas()` and `normals(per:)` give the face and vertex normals, `normals(per: .face, from: .vertex)` the face normals smoothed over shared vertices, and `Mesh.Sampler` maps points of the unit cube to places on the surface, evenly by area, from whatever source of points you choose. A face is a `Polygon<Index<Vertex>>` from swift-polygon, so triangles, quads and larger faces live side by side until you ask for `triangulated`. It holds no materials, texture coordinates or transforms: put what a vertex carries in the vertex type and use `map`. Its one model is Newell's teapot, built from his patches as other patch data would be: tessellated patch by patch and joined with `appending`; `welded` joins the vertices patches share.

## Laws

- A face is a closed cycle of at least three vertex indices, typed `Index<Vertex>` so that a vertex position cannot be mistaken for a count or for a position in another list. Construction fails with `.indexOutOfBounds` when a face names an index outside `vertices`; decoding enforces the same. Nothing else is checked: repeated indices, unused vertices, faces sharing an edge in the same direction and holes are all accepted.
- Equality and hashing compare the vertices in order and the faces as given. Two meshes that list the same surface differently are different values.
- `map` transforms the vertices in order and keeps the faces. It stops at the first failure.
- `reversed` reverses every face, keeping each face's first index, and `reversed.reversed == self`.
- `triangulated` splits each face of n indices into n − 2 triangles that fan from its first index, in face order, with the same orientation. Triangles are left as they are, so it is idempotent.
- `appending(_:)` lists this mesh's vertices and faces and then the other's, with the other's indices shifted by this mesh's vertex count. `empty` is neutral on both sides.
- `welded` merges vertices that are equal into the first of them, keeps the order of first occurrences, and renames the indices in the faces. No two vertices of the result are equal, every face still resolves to the same vertices, the face count is unchanged, and welding twice changes nothing.
- `polygon(at:)` is the face with each index replaced by its vertex. It traps for an index outside `faces`.
- `Mesh(polygons)` lists each polygon's vertices in turn and makes it one face over them, in order, so `polygon(at: i)` is polygon i, and a vertex two polygons share is listed once for each until `welded`. An empty list is `empty`.
- `triangles` is the number of triangles `triangulated` gives, n − 2 for each face of n indices, counted without building them: 12 for a box, twice the count for a mesh of quads.
- A grid of `columns` × `rows` cells has one quad per cell, listed row by row. An open direction has one more line of vertices than cells; a closed direction has as many, and its last line of cells joins the first line of vertices. Vertices are requested and stored row by row, at index `row × lines per row + column`. The quad of cell (c, r) is (c, r), (c + 1, r), (c + 1, r + 1), (c, r + 1): counterclockwise when the column direction is drawn to the right and the row direction upward. It traps unless there is at least one column and one row.
- `areas()` gives each face's vector area by Newell's method, measured from the face's first vertex so that the result does not depend on where the mesh sits: its length is the area of the face's projection onto its best plane, and it points to the side from which the face is seen counterclockwise. `normals(per: .face)` is that vector at unit length. `normals(per: .vertex)` is the unit vector of a sum over every corner of a face at the vertex: the cross product of the corner's edge to the next vertex and its edge to the previous one, divided by the product of their squared lengths (Max's weights). When the vertex and the neighbours its edges reach lie on one sphere it is that sphere's normal, up to rounding: at every vertex of `sphere`, poles included, at every corner of a cube, and on a flat surface. Weighting by area or by angle would lean where small faces meet large ones, as round a pole; the faces' vector areas, `areas()`, are there for a sum weighted by area. A corner with an edge of zero length, or a term that is not finite, adds nothing, and a zero or non-finite sum gives the zero vector, as for an unused vertex. Reversing a mesh negates every normal.
- `normals(per: .face, from: .vertex)` is, for each face in order, the unit vector of the sum of `normals(per: .vertex)` over its indices: the face's normal when a surface drawn face by face should shade smoothly. It smooths only across vertices that faces share, so a face whose vertices are its own keeps its flat normal; weld first. On a closed surface that faces outward, such as `sphere`, every such normal points outward. A zero or non-finite sum gives the zero vector. Per `.vertex` from `.face`, and from an element to itself, it is `normals(per:)` of that element.
- `torus(major:minor:segments:sides:)` is the grid closed in both directions on the torus around the z axis whose tube centre lies on the circle of radius `major` in the xy plane, with tube radius `minor`. It has `segments × sides` vertices and as many quads, is closed (every edge is used once in each direction), has Euler characteristic 0, and faces outward. Its first vertex is (`major + minor`, 0, 0).
- `sphere(radius:meridians:parallels:)` is centred on the origin with its poles on the z axis. Each pole is one vertex, so it has `meridians × (parallels − 1) + 2` vertices; the bands next to the poles are triangles and the rest quads, `meridians × parallels` faces in all. It is closed, has Euler characteristic 2, and faces outward. It traps for fewer than three meridians or two parallels.
- `box(lower:upper:)` is the box with its edges along the axes between two opposite corners. Its eight vertices are the corners in the order of a count in binary with x changing first, so vertex 0 is `lower`, vertex 1 has `upper`'s x, vertex 2 `upper`'s y, vertex 4 `upper`'s z and vertex 7 is `upper`. Its six quads are the sides toward lower x, upper x, lower y, upper y, lower z and upper z in that order. When `lower` is below `upper` along every axis it is closed, has Euler characteristic 2, twelve distinct edges, and faces outward. Nothing is checked: corners equal along an axis give a box with no depth there, whose vertices coincide in pairs.
- `cells(on:columns:rows:inset:per:including:)` takes an `Ngon<4, Point<3, Scalar>>`: columns run from its first corner toward its second and rows from its first toward its fourth, and the point at (u, v) of the face is the bilinear mix of the corners, which on a flat parallelogram is affine. `including` is asked once for every cell, row by row from row 0 and along a row from column 0, and a failure stops it. Per `.cell`, each chosen cell (c, r) gives one quad, in that order, with corners at (c + w, r + h), (c + 1 − w, r + h), (c + 1 − w, r + 1 − h) and (c + w, r + 1 − h) in cells, where `inset` is (w, h), the shares of the cell's width and height cut from each side. Per `.row`, each row with a chosen cell gives one strip at the same height, from the left of its first chosen cell's quad to the right of its last; rows with none give nothing. Every quad has four vertices of its own, so there are four times as many vertices as faces, and turns the way the face's corners do, so on a flat face its normal is the face's. No cell chosen gives `empty`. Nothing is checked: an inset outside 0 to ½ gives quads that leave their cells or turn inside out.
- `outline` is every edge that only one face uses, compared without direction, as the segment between its vertex indices. It lists them in face order, then in the order of the face's edges, each running the way its face does, so on counterclockwise faces it runs counterclockwise around the mesh and clockwise around a hole. A closed surface, such as `sphere`, `torus` or `box`, has none; a grid of c × r cells has 2(c + r); reversing a mesh reverses each of its edges.
- `disk(radius:cells:)` is the disk of `radius` about the origin of the plane: a square of half-side 0.4 `radius` cut into `cells` × `cells` quads, and four grids of as many between its sides and the circle, each side point carried along its ray from the origin onto the circle in equal steps. The point j of n along a side is at (2j − n)/n of the half-side, which is exact at both ends and opposite for j and n − j, so the grids meet at equal vertices and are welded: 5n² faces, 5n² + 2n + 1 vertices, Euler characteristic 1, every face counterclockwise, and an outline of 4n edges on the circle. Its area is below πr² and tends to it at second order. It traps for no cells.
- `Mesh(inside:around:cells:)` takes a closed outline of Bézier curves in the plane, each ending exactly where the next starts, either way round, and a centre from which every ray crosses it once. It carries each vertex v of the unit `disk` with `cells` to the point a share |v| of the way from the centre to where the ray through v crosses the outline, found on the curve by halving; the disk's outline vertices go to those crossings exactly, so the mesh's outline lies on the curves. It keeps the disk's faces, so 5 `cells`² quads, all counterclockwise and inside the outline, and its area tends to the outline's at second order. An outline of straight sides is covered exactly where its corners lie on the disk's seams, and a circle about the centre gives `disk(radius:cells:)` moved there. It fails with `.openOutline` for no curves or a gap, with `.outlineNotStarShaped` when, checked at 64 points along each curve, the outline does not turn steadily once around the centre, and with `.folded` rather than give a face that turns over or has no area.
- A `Mesh.Volume` is vertices and cells; construction fails with `.indexOutOfBounds` when a cell names an index outside `vertices`. A `Cell` has exactly eight corners, or `.notHexahedron`: the four of its lower face counterclockwise seen from above, then the four above them in the same order, the image of the cube [−1, 1]³ under the trilinear map with corner 0 at (−1, −1, −1), 1 at (1, −1, −1), 2 at (1, 1, −1), 3 at (−1, 1, −1) and 4 to 7 the same at z = 1. Its `faces` are below (0, 3, 2, 1), above (4, 5, 6, 7), then (0, 1, 5, 4), (1, 2, 6, 5), (2, 3, 7, 6), (3, 0, 4, 7), each counterclockwise seen from outside a cell turned the right way. `reversed` swaps the lower and upper four, and twice is the identity.
- `volumes()` is each cell's signed volume, the integral of the trilinear map's Jacobian determinant by the 2 × 2 × 2 Gauss rule, which is exact for it: a box, a parallelepiped or a sheared frustum gets its volume up to rounding, a cell turned the right way a positive one, its reverse the negative.
- `boundary` is the mesh, over the same vertices, of the faces only one cell uses, compared without direction or starting corner, in cell order and then `Cell.faces` order, each as its cell gives it. For every volume built here it is closed (each edge used once in each direction, no `outline`), its vector areas sum to zero, it faces outward, and the volume it encloses by the divergence theorem, each face a bilinear patch, equals the sum of `volumes()` up to rounding; the cells' vertices − edges + faces − cells is 1. `welded` merges equal vertices into the first, in the order of first occurrence.
- `Mesh.Volume(below: top, layers:, depth:)` drops each vertex of a planar mesh of quads straight down in `layers` equal steps to `depth(vertex)` below it, with the top at z = 0. Vertex i is the top's vertex i at z = 0, and layer ℓ's vertices follow in the top's order, so vertex ℓ v + i is vertex i at `depth` × ℓ / `layers` below, with the bottom at exactly −`depth`. Cells follow layer by layer from the top, so cell f of the first layer has the top's face f as its upper face, corners 4 to 7, exactly: the volume's face at the top is the given mesh, vertex for vertex. A depth linear in x and y is filled exactly, its volume the integral of the depth over the top. It fails with `.notQuadrilateral` for any face that is not a quad, and with `.folded` for a depth that is not positive and finite or a cell that turns over, as below a clockwise face. It traps for no layers.
- `Mesh.Volume.bowl(radius:cells:)` is `disk`'s layout taken into three dimensions: a half cube of half-side 0.4 `radius` and as deep, cut into `cells`³ hexahedra, and five blocks of as many carrying its four sides and its bottom outward along rays from the origin onto the sphere in equal steps, welded. It has 6 `cells`³ cells, all turned the right way; its first vertices are `disk(radius:cells:)`'s in order at z = 0 and every other lies below. Its volume is below ⅔ π r³ and tends to it at second order (1.4 % below at 8 cells). It traps for no cells.
- `Mesh.Volume(under: outline, around: centre, depth:, cells:)` carries `bowl(radius: 1, cells:)` as `Mesh(inside:around:cells:)` carries the unit disk: (x, y, z) goes to the point a share |(x, y)| of the way from `centre` to where the ray through (x, y) crosses the outline, at `depth` × z. Its first vertices are `Mesh(inside: outline, around: centre, cells: cells)`'s, in order and equal, at z = 0, and the top's faces are the boundary's faces at z = 0; the bowl meets the top at a right angle all round. Its depth at a share s along each ray is `depth` × √(1 − s²), so its volume tends to ⅔ `depth` times the outline's area, at second order. It fails as `Mesh(inside:around:cells:)` does, and with `.folded` for a depth that is not positive or a cell that turns over.
- `teapot(divisions:)` is Newell's teapot of 1975 as he drew it: thirty-two bicubic Bézier patches in the order rim, body, handle, spout, lid and an added bottom, each cut into `divisions` × `divisions` quads with (`divisions` + 1)² vertices of its own, so vertices on a shared patch edge are listed once per patch. It stands on z = 0 around the z axis, 3.15 high, every face counterclockwise seen from outside, and y → −y maps it onto itself.
- Torus and sphere vertices are computed with the scalar's own `sin` and `cos`, so they are as repeatable across platforms as those functions are.
- `Mesh(patch, columns:rows:interpolating:)` is the open grid whose vertex at (column, row) is `patch.value(at: column / columns, row / rows, interpolating:)`. Its corner vertices are the patch's corner control points and its boundary vertices lie on the patch's boundary curves, exactly, when the interpolation returns its endpoints exactly at 0 and 1. Patches that share a boundary curve in the same direction therefore produce equal vertices along it, which `welded` joins.
- `Mesh.Sampler(_:)` cuts each face into the triangles fanned from its first vertex, (0, 1, 2), (0, 2, 3) and so on, and adds up their areas once, in face order; `area` is the total, and the sampler is `nil` for a mesh whose total is not positive and finite. `location(_:_:_:)` holds each of u, v and w to 0…1. u times the area picks the first triangle whose running area reaches it, by halving; (v, w) place it on that triangle as weights of the face's vertices in face order, which are not negative and add up to one: 1 − a − b on the first vertex, a and b on the triangle's other two corners, 0 elsewhere, with (a, b) = (v, w), or (1 − v, 1 − w) when v + w > 1. Points spread evenly over the unit cube therefore give places spread evenly over the triangles, whatever the shape of the faces; a face that is not flat is sampled as its fan. `position(of:)` and `interpolate(_:at:)` sum the face's vertex values times the weights in face order; an interpolated normal is not of unit length in general. The same three coordinates give the same place on every platform, as far as the scalar's arithmetic is the same.
- A mesh encodes as `vertices` and `faces`, each face a bare array of indices.
